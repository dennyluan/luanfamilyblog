---
title: Thoughts on Shanghai so far
pubDate: '2025-09-20'
---

Being Chinese is like riding a bike. Once you've done it, it's just a skill that comes back. Also like riding a bike, if you're rusty because of not riding for a while, then you'll look a bit ungraceful at first. This was me when I was riding under the trees of the former French concession on the ride share app bike. It cost me $0.20 USD to get to the coffee shop where I am writing this now.

For a long time, I imagined that once I got to China, there would be a moment where I would be so relieved to finally be here, to be overcome with emotion, that I would break down and cry. I would bask in the noise of a different language, the hot sun, and feel such a massive relief. We've been here for two weeks now, plus another two weeks in Taiwan before this, and that moment never came. Really, what I think I was feeling was such an aggressive urge to leave America. To escape.

Instead, the whole time we've been here I feel like I've been just trying to keep up! The first week was spent getting us situated as quickly as possible, which meant baby proofing an old apartment by ordering things off Taobao. Then it was just finding new food and restaraunts to eat.

For a while, we kept getting stuck in the nearby malls around Jingan temple, which is where we spent most of our time. Malls in Shanghai are luxurious and glitzy hubs of mass convenience. The bottom floor of one has an upscale grocery store combined with a bustling food court filled with options - Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Sichuan, burgers, philly cheese steaks, desserts and sweets. We like the neighboring mall because their bottom floor food court has a lot of good spots. We've had Lanzhou hand-pulled noodles, and Yunnan rice noodles, and some other kinds of noodles. It wasn't until like 10 days later that we actually ventured up to the 3rd floor, and realized that there 6 other floors full of diverse and busy restaraunts, including a massive hot pot buffet on the top floor. We had just been enamored and distracted by the bottom floor food courts the whole time thinking we'd found all that the mall could offer.

This story is a good analogy for our time here so far. There's so many layers that just keep surprising me that I haven't had much time to bask in the "I was right" gloating of telling Cindy we should move here for over a year.

Other things that have changed or that we've noticed since being here:
  - Even more convienient than two years ago: Amazon Prime feels like a relic of a previous decade.
  - Raw materials are insanely cheap, because they all come from here. In Taiwan, Mosburger labels that their meat comes from New Zealand. In China, Luckin ads say their apples come from Xinjiang.
  - 文明++, sometimes walking around you could tell me in Tokyo, based on people's behavior and environment, and I wouldn't question it.
  - All of the Silicon Valley ideas that never took off in America work here: QR codes, locker boxes, cheap food delivery, public transit. Why is America so resistant to change?
  - Taiwan feels stuck in time, Japan does too.
  - China is sweating the details now in a way that feels new and different. There's no more paper construction, shoddy quality fears, and now you can find attention to detail and craftsmanship. Obviously there's still a large range, but going into a Huawei store on our second day dispelled the worry instantly.
  - Before getting here, I was somewhat worried about scams. I'm still on guard, but it seems like I'm the only one who is.
  - Summer heat is bad, but not too bad. We can still go out and do things with Ollie strapped to us, and cold AC is everywhere.
  - I can instantly tell when someone is an ABC tourist. It's a fun game, but the recent Chinese meme of ABCs having a different "air" about them is so true, and makes me curious if people can tell with us.
  - Shanghai still feels very international, about the same as Taipei if not more. Lots of 外国人 locals.
  - Smoking still very prevalent, my guess is that even more so outside of major cities where there's a big push to reduce smoking.

My only complaint so far is a big one, which is the internet. I've still had to work while being here, and this work is the only thing keeping me connected to the Western internet. I think that if we actually weren't still tied to outside internet, then we wouldn't have to deal with this problem at all. Decoupling fully and living entirely inside the firewall feels easily doable and something we're ready for, but unfortunately isn't an option for us yet. If China did relax the firewall, I would move here permanently in a heartbeat.

Most of these initial reactions admittedly are just about consumption and notices about daily life, and admittedly we haven't delved very deep outside of the city bubble. I hope to explore around Jiangsu and Zhejiang soon.
